## Cisplatin Discovery & Chemistry

### 1. Rosenberg B, Van Camp L, Krigas T. (1965). Inhibition of cell division in Escherichia coli by electrolysis products from a platinum electrode. *Nature*, 205(4972):698–699.
- **DOI:** 10.1038/205698a0
- **PMID:** 14287410
- **Relevance:** Foundational serendipitous discovery — cisplatin origin story
- **Key finding:** During electrolysis experiments studying bacterial growth in electric fields, Rosenberg et al. observed that platinum electrolysis products caused *E. coli* to form elongated filaments up to 300× normal length, halting cell division without inhibiting growth. This platinum-induced cell-division arrest was the first demonstration of the biological activity that would lead to cisplatin. The phenomenon was traceable to cis-diamminedichloroplatinum(II) and related amine platinum complexes generated at the electrode.
- **Why it matters:** This is the founding observation of platinum anticancer biology. Every mechanistic paper on cisplatin traces its lineage to this 1965 communication. Essential citation for any historical section of a cisplatin review.

### 2. Rosenberg B, Van Camp L, Trosko JE, Mansour VH. (1969). Platinum compounds: a new class of potent antitumour agents. *Nature*, 222(5191):385–386.
- **DOI:** 10.1038/222385a0
- **PMID:** 5782119
- **Relevance:** First in vivo antitumor efficacy of cisplatin; chemical class established
- **Key finding:** Cisplatin (cis-diamminedichloroplatinum(II)) and related cis-platinum amine complexes strongly inhibited sarcoma 180 and leukaemia L1210 tumors in mice; trans-isomers were inactive, establishing the critical importance of cis-geometry for antitumor activity. This stereoselectivity foreshadowed the molecular basis (cis-configured adducts on DNA).
- **Why it matters:** The companion paper to the 1965 discovery, demonstrating in vivo efficacy and the cis/trans selectivity rule. These two Nature papers together are the canonical pair for the "cisplatin discovery" narrative and explain why aquation must maintain cis configuration.

### 3. Kelland L. (2007). The resurgence of platinum-based cancer chemotherapy. *Nature Reviews Cancer*, 7(8):573–584.
- **DOI:** 10.1038/nrc2167
- **PMID:** 17625587
- **Relevance:** Cisplatin chemistry overview; aquation; resistance mechanisms; second/third-generation analogues
- **Key finding:** Cisplatin undergoes stepwise aquation in low-chloride intracellular environments (replacing Cl− with H₂O), forming reactive mono- and diaqua species that bind DNA. The review surveys how structural modifications to the platinum scaffold (carboplatin, oxaliplatin, satraplatin, picoplatin) alter pharmacokinetics, toxicity profiles, and tumor resistance profiles. The clinical rationale for each analogue is mechanistically grounded.
- **Why it matters:** The most comprehensive single-article reference for cisplatin chemistry, clinical use, and mechanistic resistance — essential for situating the drug within broader platinum pharmacology for a targeted review audience.

## Cellular Uptake (CTR1, OCT2, ATP7A/B)

### 4. Ishida S, Lee J, Thiele DJ, Herskowitz I. (2002). Uptake of the anticancer drug cisplatin mediated by the copper transporter Ctr1 in yeast and mammals. *PNAS*, 99(22):14298–14302.
- **DOI:** 10.1073/pnas.162491399
- **PMID:** 12370430
- **Relevance:** CTR1 copper transporter as primary cisplatin influx route
- **Key finding:** Deletion of the *CTR1* gene (high-affinity copper importer) in both yeast and mammalian cells resulted in significantly reduced intracellular cisplatin accumulation and increased drug resistance. Copper exposure, which triggers CTR1 protein degradation and internalization, similarly reduced cisplatin uptake. Conversely, cisplatin itself caused CTR1 degradation and interfered with copper uptake, revealing substrate competition.
- **Why it matters:** Established the molecular identity of the primary cisplatin influx transporter. Explains why copper levels modulate cisplatin efficacy and why CTR1 loss is a resistance mechanism — directly relevant to the uptake section.

### 5. Ciarimboli G, Ludwig T, Lang D, Pavenstädt H, Koepsell H, Piechota HJ, Haier J, Jutting U, Grabenbauer G, Schlatter E. (2005). Organic cation transporter 2 mediates cisplatin-induced oto- and nephrotoxicity and is a target for protective interventions. *American Journal of Pathology*, 167(6):1477–1484.
- **DOI:** 10.1016/S0002-9440(10)61234-5
- **PMID:** 16314464
- **Relevance:** OCT2/SLC22A2 as the organ-specific transporter driving nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity
- **Key finding:** OCT2 (encoded by *SLC22A2*), expressed at the basolateral membrane of renal proximal tubule cells and in cochlear cells, mediates preferential cisplatin accumulation in these tissues. Inhibition of OCT2 with cimetidine or probenecid reduced cisplatin uptake and protected against nephrotoxicity without impairing antitumor efficacy. Carboplatin is not an OCT2 substrate, explaining its relative nephrotoxic sparing.
- **Why it matters:** Provides the molecular explanation for the organ specificity of cisplatin toxicity. Critical for the toxicity-mechanism section and foundational to the chronotherapy rationale (OCT2 expression/activity may be circadian-gated).

### 6. ATP7A/B efflux — Katano K, Safaei R, Samimi G, Holzer A, Tomioka M, Goodman M, Howell SB. (2003). Confocal microscopic analysis of the interaction between cisplatin and the copper transporter ATP7B in human ovarian carcinoma cells. *Clinical Cancer Research*, 9(16):6128–6134.
- **DOI:** Not available in PubMed record (PMID: 14613995)
- **PMID:** 14613995
- **Relevance:** ATP7A/ATP7B P-type ATPase copper efflux pumps sequester and export cisplatin
- **Key finding:** Cisplatin exposure triggers redistribution of ATP7B from the trans-Golgi network to cytoplasmic vesicles near the plasma membrane, consistent with an active vesicular efflux mechanism. ATP7B overexpression in ovarian carcinoma cells conferred resistance; siRNA knockdown of ATP7B restored cisplatin sensitivity up to 10.6-fold. ATP7A showed similar but distinct spatiotemporal dynamics.
- **Why it matters:** Completes the uptake-efflux axis: CTR1 governs import, ATP7A/B govern export. Both arms are relevant to resistance mechanisms and potential chronopharmacological modulation.

*Note: A canonical companion reference is Samimi G et al. (2004) Mol Cancer Ther 3:1543–1549 (PMID: 15634648), confirming ATP7A role in cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer.*

## DNA Adduct Formation

### 7. Wang D, Lippard SJ. (2005). Cellular processing of platinum anticancer drugs. *Nature Reviews Drug Discovery*, 4(4):307–320.
- **DOI:** 10.1038/nrd1691
- **PMID:** 15789122
- **Relevance:** Complete mechanistic review: aquation → DNA binding → adduct types → signaling → apoptosis
- **Key finding:** After entering the cell, cisplatin undergoes aquation (Cl⁻ → H₂O exchange) in the low-chloride cytoplasm (~4 mM vs. ~100 mM extracellular), generating reactive electrophilic species. These react preferentially with N7 of guanine, forming 1,2-d(GpG) intrastrand crosslinks (~65% of adducts), 1,2-d(ApG) intrastrand crosslinks (~25%), 1,3-intrastrand adducts (~6%), and interstrand crosslinks (~2%). Each adduct class distorts the DNA duplex differently, with the major 1,2-GpG adduct bending DNA ~32–35° toward the major groove and unwinding the helix by ~13°.
- **Why it matters:** The definitive mechanistic reference encompassing all steps from drug entry to cell fate. Provides the molecular architecture linking cisplatin chemistry to downstream NER and apoptosis — the backbone citation for Section 2.

### 8. Jung Y, Lippard SJ. (2007). Direct cellular responses to platinum-induced DNA damage. *Chemical Reviews*, 107(5):1387–1407.
- **DOI:** 10.1021/cr068207j
- **PMID:** 17455916
- **Relevance:** Protein recognition of platinum-DNA adducts; HMG-domain proteins; downstream signaling
- **Key finding:** Multiple classes of cellular proteins recognize platinum-DNA adducts: HMG-domain proteins (HMGB1, hSRY) bind 1,2-intrastrand crosslinks with high affinity and may shield adducts from NER; mismatch repair proteins (MSH2–MSH6) recognize adducts and trigger apoptosis in a futile repair cycle; replication machinery stalls at adducts generating ssDNA gaps that activate ATR. The review catalogs the full protein interactome of platinum-DNA lesions and maps recognition events to downstream pathway activation.
- **Why it matters:** Bridges adduct chemistry to cell biology by cataloging all major cellular proteins that read cisplatin-DNA lesions. Essential for explaining how adducts translate into NER engagement, checkpoint activation, and apoptosis.

## NER / XPA

### 9. Zamble DB, Lippard SJ. (1995). Cisplatin and DNA repair in cancer chemotherapy. *Trends in Biochemical Sciences*, 20(10):435–439.
- **DOI:** 10.1016/S0968-0004(00)89095-7
- **PMID:** 8533159
- **Relevance:** Foundational NER-cisplatin link; XPA-mediated damage recognition; NER as resistance driver
- **Key finding:** Cisplatin 1,2-intrastrand adducts are the principal cytotoxic lesions, and nucleotide excision repair (NER) is the dominant pathway for their removal. XPA protein binds bent, damaged DNA at adduct sites and recruits the ERCC1–XPF endonuclease complex to incise the damaged strand. Enhanced NER capacity in tumor cells directly correlates with cisplatin resistance. The review identified that the relative insensitivity of testicular germ-cell tumors to NER (low XPA levels) explains their exceptional cisplatin sensitivity.
- **Why it matters:** The foundational paper linking NER efficiency to clinical cisplatin response. Identified XPA as the critical damage-recognition scaffold — now a validated biomarker for platinum sensitivity.

### 10. Structural insights into the recognition of cisplatin lesions by XPA — Sugitani N, Sivley RM, Paul KE, Capra JA, Chazin WJ. (2016). XPA: A key scaffold for human nucleotide excision repair. *DNA Repair*, 44:123–135.
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.dnarep.2016.05.018
- **PMID:** 27247238
- **Relevance:** XPA structural mechanism of cisplatin adduct recognition and NER scaffold assembly
- **Key finding:** XPA contains a zinc-finger domain that directly contacts kinked, platinated DNA, preferentially binding bent duplexes bearing 1,2-GpG cisplatin adducts. XPA acts as the central scaffold that recruits RPA (to stabilize ssDNA), TFIIH (for bidirectional helicase unwinding), and the ERCC1–XPF/XPG endonucleases. XPA-deficient cells cannot excise cisplatin adducts and are hypersensitive to the drug.
- **Why it matters:** Provides the molecular structure-function basis for the XPA step of NER, explaining why low-XPA tumors (testicular cancer) are hypersensitive to cisplatin while high-XPA tumors (NSCLC) are resistant.

## DNA Damage Response (ATR/ATM → p53)

### 11. Pabla N, Huang S, Mi QS, Daniel R, Dong Z. (2008). ATR-Chk2 signaling in p53 activation and DNA damage response during cisplatin-induced apoptosis. *Journal of Biological Chemistry*, 283(10):6572–6583.
- **DOI:** 10.1074/jbc.M707568200
- **PMID:** 18162465
- **Relevance:** ATR → CHK2 → p53 activation axis specifically during cisplatin injury
- **Key finding:** In renal proximal tubule cells treated with cisplatin, ATR (not ATM or DNA-PK) is the primary kinase activated, co-localizing with phosphorylated H2AX at nuclear foci. ATR activates CHK2, which phosphorylates and stabilizes p53. Dominant-negative ATR blocked cisplatin-induced p53 activation and apoptosis. The study demonstrated a linear ATR → CHK2 → p53 pathway mediating cisplatin nephrotoxicity, distinct from the UV-damage ATR → CHK1 canonical pathway.
- **Why it matters:** Defines the specific kinase cascade activated by cisplatin-DNA adducts in clinically relevant (nephrotoxicity) cell types, linking DNA damage sensing to the apoptotic executor p53 — bridging the NER and apoptosis subsections.

## Apoptosis (p53–PUMA–BAX)

### 12. Florea AM, Büsselberg D. (2011). Cisplatin as an anti-tumor drug: cellular mechanisms of activity, drug resistance and induced side effects. *Cancers*, 3(1):1351–1371.
- **DOI:** 10.3390/cancers3011351
- **PMID:** 24212665
- **Relevance:** Comprehensive apoptosis pathway review: p53 → PUMA/PIDD → BAX/BAK → MOMP → caspase cascade
- **Key finding:** Cisplatin-induced apoptosis proceeds via the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway: DNA damage signals activate p53, which transcriptionally upregulates *PUMA* and *PIDD*; PUMA displaces BAX from BCL-2/BCL-XL, enabling BAX/BAK oligomerization and mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP); cytochrome c release activates caspase-9 and effector caspase-3/7. A parallel p53-independent pathway, involving calcium-dependent signaling and direct mitochondrial targeting, operates in p53-null cells.
- **Why it matters:** Provides a complete mechanistic narrative of the apoptosis cascade downstream of cisplatin-DNA damage, covering both p53-dependent and independent routes — essential for explaining why cell death is not guaranteed by DNA adduct formation alone.

### 13. Cepeda V, Fuertes MA, Castilla J, Alonso C, Quevedo C, Pérez JM. (2007). Biochemical mechanisms of cisplatin cytotoxicity. *Anti-Cancer Agents in Medicinal Chemistry*, 7(1):3–18.
- **DOI:** 10.2174/187152007779314044
- **PMID:** 17266502
- **Relevance:** p53–BAX axis; Bcl-2 family balance; PUMA induction; death receptor cross-talk
- **Key finding:** Cisplatin activates both the intrinsic (mitochondrial) and extrinsic (death-receptor/FasL) apoptotic pathways, with their relative contributions varying by cell type and p53 status. The BCL-2:BAX ratio is a critical determinant of cisplatin sensitivity; PUMA and NOXA (both p53 targets) are the principal BH3-only proteins that initiate BAX activation. Cisplatin also induces reactive oxygen species generation, which amplifies the apoptotic signal upstream of mitochondria.
- **Why it matters:** Complements the Florea/Büsselberg review by detailing the upstream regulators (ROS, BCL-2 family balance, death receptors) that set the apoptotic threshold — mechanistically important for understanding why some head-and-neck cancers respond and others do not.
