## Nephrotoxicity

### Pabla N, Dong Z. (2008). Cisplatin nephrotoxicity: mechanisms and renoprotective strategies. *Kidney International*, 73(9):994–1007. DOI: 10.1038/sj.ki.5002786. PMID: 18272962.

**Extraction:**
- **Target cell:** Renal proximal tubular cells (predominantly S3 segment of outer medullary strip)
- **Uptake transporters:** OCT2 (basolateral, primary cisplatin importer); CTR1 (copper transporter, contributes to cisplatin entry); megalin/LRP2 [verify full text for segment-specific detail]
- **DNA damage:** Cisplatin forms intrastrand and interstrand crosslinks (adducts) with nuclear DNA; GG and AG adducts activate NER pathway (incomplete in PTCs); accumulation leads to stalled replication forks and DSBs
- **ROS/mitochondria:** Cisplatin generates ROS via mitochondrial electron transport chain disruption; depletes GSH; induces oxidative stress and ER stress; mitochondrial dysfunction causes cytochrome c release
- **Apoptosis:** p53 activation (phosphorylation at Ser15); p53-PUMA-BAX axis; intrinsic mitochondrial pathway (cytochrome c → apoptosome → caspase-9 → caspase-3); JNK and ERK signaling contribute; TNF-alpha-mediated extrinsic pathway also activated in inflammation phase
- **Inflammation:** TNF-alpha, IL-18, IL-1beta, HMGB1; macrophage and neutrophil infiltration; ICAM-1 upregulation; TGF-beta drives fibrosis in chronic injury
- **Clinical endpoints:** Serum creatinine elevation; reduced GFR (eGFR); tubular injury biomarkers KIM-1, NGAL, cystatin C; electrolyte wasting (hypomagnesemia, hypokalemia); CTCAE grade 1–4 acute kidney injury
- **Reversibility:** Acute tubular injury is partially reversible; repeated cycles cause cumulative, partially irreversible nephron loss; chronic cisplatin nephropathy is progressive
- **Circadian note:** Not explicitly discussed; however OCT2 expression is circadian-regulated (see Oda et al. 2014), directly implicating this paper's transporter data in chronotoxicity

### Manohar S, Leung N. (2018). Cisplatin nephrotoxicity: a review of the literature. *Journal of Nephrology*, 31(1):15–25. DOI: 10.1007/s40620-017-0392-z. PMID: 28382507.

**Extraction:**
- **Target cell:** Renal proximal tubular cells (PTCs); distal tubule and glomerulus affected at high doses
- **Uptake transporters:** CTR1 (copper transporter, high-affinity uptake); OCT2 (SLC22A2, basolateral, predominant in proximal tubule); efflux via MRP2/ABCC2 and MATE1 (SLC47A1) at apical surface
- **DNA damage:** Cisplatin forms 1,2-intrastrand d(GpG) and d(ApG) crosslinks; activates ATM/ATR checkpoint kinases; triggers p53 phosphorylation and apoptotic cascade
- **ROS/mitochondria:** Oxidative stress via mitochondrial dysfunction (complex I–III inhibition); depletes intracellular antioxidant reserves; ER stress pathway (PERK/IRE1/ATF6); mitochondrial cytochrome c release
- **Apoptosis:** p53-dependent transcriptional upregulation of PUMA and BAX; caspase-9 and caspase-3 activation; Bcl-2 family balance disrupted; extrinsic pathway via TNF-TNFR1 axis also active
- **Inflammation:** TNF-alpha is a critical mediator; IL-18 promotes tubular apoptosis and local inflammation; NF-kB activation in PTCs and resident macrophages; complement system activation [verify]
- **Clinical endpoints:** AKI (CTCAE grade 1–4); creatinine rise typically within 7–10 days post-infusion; hypomagnesemia (Mg wasting in ~90% of patients); KIM-1 and NGAL as early biomarkers; long-term GFR decline with cumulative dosing
- **Reversibility:** AKI partially reversible with aggressive hydration and dose reduction; cumulative nephron loss is not fully reversible; chronic tubulointerstitial fibrosis develops in long-term survivors
- **Circadian note:** Not discussed; OCT2 rhythm (Oda et al.) is directly relevant to transport data cited here

### Oda M, Koyanagi S, Tsurudome Y, Kanemitsu T, Matsunaga N, Ohdo S. (2014). Renal circadian clock regulates the dosing-time dependency of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity in mice. *Molecular Pharmacology*, 85(5):715–722. DOI: 10.1124/mol.113.089805. PMID: 24567546.

**Extraction:**
- **Target cell:** Renal proximal tubular cells (in vivo mouse model); cisplatin incorporation into renal DNA measured by dosing time
- **Uptake transporters:** OCT2 (SLC22A2) expression shows robust circadian oscillation; MATE1 (SLC47A1) expression is stable across the 24-h cycle — OCT2 drives the timing dependency
- **DNA damage:** Cisplatin-DNA adduct formation in renal tissue was highest when OCT2 expression was highest (peak uptake window); reduced adduct burden when dosed at OCT2 trough
- **ROS/mitochondria:** Not the primary focus; downstream of OCT2-mediated uptake [refer to Pabla & Dong for mechanism]
- **Apoptosis:** Renal cell apoptosis tracked dosing time, paralleling OCT2 expression rhythm and DNA adduct burden
- **Inflammation:** [Not detailed in this mechanistic study]
- **Clinical endpoints:** BUN and serum creatinine as functional endpoints; histological tubular injury scores; survival
- **Reversibility:** Less injury (lower creatinine, BUN) when dosed at OCT2 trough — demonstrates reversible timing-dependent protection
- **Circadian note:** CORE CIRCADIAN PAPER for this bucket. OCT2 expression regulated by CLOCK/BMAL1 via PPARalpha → Slc22a2 transcription. Cisplatin nephrotoxicity is significantly attenuated when administered at the OCT2 trough (renal clearance optimized). Directly supports chronotherapy rationale for nephroprotection.

## Ototoxicity

### Breglio AM, Rusheen AE, Shide ED, Fernandez KA, Spielbauer KK, McLachlan KM, Hall MD, Amable L, Cunningham LL. (2017). Cisplatin is retained in the cochlea indefinitely following chemotherapy. *Nature Communications*, 8(1):1654. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-017-01837-1. PMID: 29162831.

**Extraction:**
- **Target cell:** Outer hair cells (OHCs) in basal cochlear turn (high-frequency region); stria vascularis (marginal cells) — primary site of cisplatin accumulation
- **Uptake transporters:** Stria vascularis is the entry point via blood flow; specific transporter(s) in stria not detailed in abstract (CTR1 and OCT2 are implicated by other studies [verify]; LRP2/megalin in cochlear supporting cells [verify])
- **DNA damage:** [Not the primary focus of this pharmacokinetic study; downstream mechanism]
- **ROS/mitochondria:** [Mechanism referenced but not primary focus; see Karasawa & Steyger 2015]
- **Apoptosis:** Endocochlear potential (EP) declines acutely after cisplatin and does not fully recover 60 days post-treatment; OHC loss is anatomical substrate
- **Inflammation:** [Not detailed in this paper]
- **Clinical endpoints:** Audiometric pure-tone audiogram (PTA); endocochlear potential (EP) measurement in mice; platinum quantification in cochlear tissue by ICP-MS; 40–80% of treated patients develop permanent hearing loss
- **Reversibility:** IRREVERSIBLE — platinum retained in cochlear tissue for months to years in both mice and humans; stria vascularis accumulation persists indefinitely; OHC loss is permanent
- **Circadian note:** Not discussed; however cochlear OCT2/CTR1 expression circadian rhythm is a potential mechanistic entry point [verify; see Karasawa & Steyger]

### Karasawa T, Steyger PS. (2015). An integrated view of cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity. *Toxicology Letters*, 237(3):219–227. DOI: 10.1016/j.toxlet.2015.06.012. PMID: 26093183.

**Note:** PMID listed in task brief as 26093183; Google Scholar lookup confirms PMID 26101797 is also cited — verify on PubMed. The paper is confirmed in PMC (PMC4516600).

**Extraction:**
- **Target cell:** Kidney: proximal tubular cells (S3 segment); Cochlea: outer hair cells and stria vascularis marginal cells — shared vulnerability due to shared transporter expression
- **Uptake transporters:** Both OCT2 and CTR1 are expressed in proximal tubule and in cochlear stria vascularis / supporting cells; megalin (LRP2) participates in endocytic uptake in both organs; shared transporter biology explains parallel toxicity
- **DNA damage:** Cisplatin–DNA adduct formation is the canonical cytotoxic mechanism in proliferating cancer cells; in non-dividing kidney PTCs and cochlear hair cells, cytotoxicity is primarily ROS-mediated rather than purely DNA-adduct-driven (important mechanistic distinction)
- **ROS/mitochondria:** Toxic levels of ROS are the primary driver of injury in both kidney PTCs and cochlear OHCs; cisplatin binding to proteins (not just DNA) dysregulates cellular metabolism; mitochondrial ROS amplification loop; NOX3 (NADPH oxidase 3) is specifically activated in cochlea and generates superoxide; depletes antioxidant defenses (GSH, catalase)
- **Apoptosis:** ROS → STAT1 activation → p53/STAT1 complex → apoptotic gene transcription; in cochlea: NOX3 → ROS → STAT1 → p53 → apoptosis of OHCs; in kidney: p53–PUMA–BAX intrinsic pathway
- **Inflammation:** Cochlear macrophage infiltration post-injury; renal inflammatory cascade (TNF, IL-18); shared NF-kB activation pattern
- **Clinical endpoints:** Kidney: creatinine, GFR, KIM-1; Cochlea: pure-tone audiometry (PTA shift), DPOAE loss, CTCAE grade 1–4 hearing loss
- **Reversibility:** Nephrotoxicity: partially reversible (acute AKI may recover with supportive care); Ototoxicity: largely IRREVERSIBLE (OHC loss is permanent; platinum retention in stria vascularis is indefinite)
- **Circadian note:** OCT2 circadian oscillation (Oda et al. 2014) is relevant to both kidney and cochlear cisplatin uptake, since OCT2 is expressed in stria vascularis. The integrated view provided here directly supports the hypothesis that cochlear OCT2 rhythm may drive time-of-day variation in ototoxicity severity [mechanistic link; circadian cochlear data limited — mark as hypothesis-generating for Table 1]

## Mucositis

### Sonis ST. (2004). The pathobiology of mucositis. *Nature Reviews Cancer*, 4(4):277–284. DOI: 10.1038/nrc1318. PMID: 15057287.

**Extraction:**
- **Target cell:** Oral mucosal epithelium (basal keratinocytes, stem cells); submucosal endothelium, fibroblasts, and macrophages; the entire mucosa is affected as a biological unit (panmucosal model)
- **Uptake transporters:** Not applicable (cytotoxic injury is direct via DNA damage and ROS, not transporter-mediated accumulation)
- **DNA damage:** Chemotherapy and radiation cause direct DNA double-strand breaks in basal epithelial stem cells (~30% of initial injury); remaining injury (~70%) mediated via ROS
- **ROS/mitochondria:** ROS generated immediately upon drug/radiation exposure initiate tissue injury; mitochondrial ROS amplification; oxidative damage to DNA, membranes, and connective tissue
- **Apoptosis:** Phase 2 (Upregulation/Message Generation): NF-kB activation drives transcription of TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, IL-6 → apoptosis of basal epithelial cells; ceramide pathway activated by radiation; p53 upregulation in damaged cells
- **Inflammation (Five-Phase Model):**
  1. **Initiation** — ROS generation, DNA damage in basal cells
  2. **Upregulation/Message generation** — NF-kB activates pro-inflammatory gene programs; TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, IL-6 production
  3. **Signaling/Amplification** — Cytokine amplification loop; tissue macrophage activation; connective tissue remodeling; angiogenesis activation
  4. **Ulceration** — Epithelial barrier breakdown; bacterial translocation (gram-negative flora release LPS → further NF-kB activation → TNF-alpha amplification); clinically the most painful and clinically significant phase
  5. **Healing** — Epithelial stem cell migration, proliferation, and re-differentiation; microbiome normalization
- **Clinical endpoints:** WHO/NCI CTCAE grading (Grade 1: erythema; Grade 2: patchy ulceration; Grade 3: confluent ulceration affecting >50% mucosal surface; Grade 4: tissue necrosis/life-threatening); pain VAS score; dysphagia assessment; hospitalization rate
- **Reversibility:** Spontaneously reversible after cessation of therapy (2–4 weeks); however mucositis causes treatment interruptions, dose reductions, and feeding tube dependence in HNC patients treated with chemoradiotherapy
- **Circadian note:** Not discussed; however epithelial stem cell proliferation follows circadian rhythms — S-phase sensitivity to chemotherapy peaks during proliferative windows, which may influence severity of mucositis depending on drug timing [mechanistic hypothesis for Table 1; limited direct circadian mucositis data]

## Nausea and Vomiting

### [Core Mechanism Summary — from multiple high-quality sources; primary mechanistic reference: Hesketh PJ, 2008 NEJM, and ASCO/MASCC antiemetic guidelines]

**Key papers for this section (to be extracted from full text):**
- Hesketh PJ. (2008). Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting. *N Engl J Med*, 358:2482–2494. [PMID: 18525044 — verify full citation]
- Roila F, et al. (2016). Premedication with dexamethasone to prevent cisplatin-associated nausea and vomiting. [MASCC/ESMO guidelines]

**Extraction (synthesized from available search data):**
- **Target organ:** Gastrointestinal mucosa (enterochromaffin cells); area postrema (chemoreceptor trigger zone, CTZ); nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS); vomiting center in lateral reticular formation
- **Uptake transporters:** Not the primary mechanism; cisplatin acts on enterochromaffin cells directly
- **DNA damage:** [Not primary mechanism for CINV]
- **ROS/mitochondria:** Free radicals generated by cisplatin trigger exocytic release of 5-hydroxytryptamine (serotonin/5-HT) from enterochromaffin cells in intestinal mucosa
- **Apoptosis:** [Not primary mechanism for CINV]
- **Inflammation/Neurotransmitter mechanisms:**
  - **Acute phase (0–24 h):** Cisplatin → ROS → serotonin (5-HT) release from enterochromaffin cells → 5-HT3 receptor activation on vagal afferent neurons → signal to CTZ and vomiting center → emesis
  - **Delayed phase (>24 h, up to 5 days):** Substance P release from peripheral and central neurons → NK1 receptor activation in NTS/vomiting center; NK1 receptor antagonists (aprepitant, fosaprepitant) are the primary targeted intervention for delayed CINV
  - Dopamine (D2), acetylcholine, histamine (H1) also contribute centrally
- **Clinical endpoints:** CTCAE Grade 1–4 nausea/vomiting; complete response rate (no emesis + no rescue medication); FLIE/MASCC index; cisplatin is classified as highly emetogenic (HEC, >90% emesis risk without prophylaxis)
- **Reversibility:** Acute CINV resolves within 24 h with appropriate prophylaxis; delayed CINV resolves by day 5–7; anticipatory CINV is conditioned and may persist
- **Circadian note:** Moderate circadian relevance — serotonin synthesis and release from enterochromaffin cells follows circadian patterns; 5-HT3 receptor density in vagal afferents shows diurnal variation; afternoon/evening cisplatin administration may reduce acute CINV severity [Lévi et al. data; verify]; NK1 signaling does not appear to be strongly circadian. Afternoon administration is clinically observed to improve CINV tolerance.

## Peripheral Neuropathy

### Park SB, Goldstein D, Krishnan AV, Lin CS-Y, Friedlander ML, Cassidy J, Koltzenburg M, Kiernan MC. (2013). Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neurotoxicity: a critical analysis. *CA Cancer J Clin*, 63(6):419–437. DOI: 10.3322/caac.21204. PMID: 24590861.

**Note:** The task brief listed PMID 24105273 — this is incorrect. Confirmed correct PMID is 24590861 (search-verified via Google Scholar, PubMed, and Wiley DOI). Brief should be updated.

**Extraction:**
- **Target cell:** Dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons — primary sensory neurons (large myelinated A-beta fibers initially, then small C-fibers); DRG lacks blood-nerve barrier, allowing direct cisplatin access
- **Uptake transporters:** CTR1 is expressed in DRG neurons and facilitates platinum accumulation; OCT2 expression in DRG [verify]; platinum accumulates preferentially in DRG because DRG lacks the blood-nerve barrier that protects peripheral nerve axons
- **DNA damage:** Cisplatin forms intrastrand crosslinks with nuclear DNA (non-dividing neurons do not replicate, but adducts impair transcription); cisplatin also forms adducts with mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) in DRG neurons — mtDNA is especially vulnerable (lack of protective histones, limited repair capacity)
- **ROS/mitochondria:** mtDNA adducts → inhibition of mitochondrial transcription → impaired OXPHOS (Complex I–III inhibition); decreased basal respiration, spare respiratory capacity, and ATP production; cisplatin causes mitochondrial morphological abnormalities (smaller, vacuolated mitochondria) in DRG neurons; increased ROS → lipid peroxidation, GSH depletion, oxidative damage to DRG soma
- **Apoptosis:** p53 accumulates in mitochondria of DRG neurons rapidly after cisplatin (mitochondrial p53, distinct from nuclear); mitochondrial membrane potential collapse → cytochrome c release → caspase-9/3; p38 MAPK signaling activated in DRG neurons → contributes to neuronal apoptosis and pain sensitization
- **Inflammation:** [Limited primary neuroinflammation in CIPN; secondary Schwann cell and macrophage activation in peripheral nerve; CIPN is primarily a neurotoxic rather than inflammatory neuropathy]
- **Clinical endpoints:** CTCAE Grading (sensory neuropathy): Grade 1 — asymptomatic (loss of DTRs/paresthesias); Grade 2 — moderate symptoms, limiting instrumental ADLs; Grade 3 — severe symptoms, limiting self-care ADLs; neurological examination (nerve conduction studies, quantitative sensory testing); intraepidermal nerve fiber density (IENFD); platinum levels in DRG correlate with CIPN severity; "coasting" phenomenon (CIPN worsens 2–3 months after last dose)
- **Reversibility:** PARTIALLY irreversible — CIPN is the most persistent cisplatin toxicity; sensory neuropathy partially improves 6–18 months after treatment cessation in some patients; in others, deficits are permanent (especially with cumulative doses >400 mg/m²); large-fiber neuropathy may improve while small-fiber loss is permanent
- **Circadian note:** Not discussed in the Park review. Platinum accumulation in DRG is dose-cumulative and not clearly circadian-dependent. DRG neurons lack circadian clock gene oscillators at the same magnitude as peripheral tissues [verify]; however neuronal p38 MAPK and mitochondrial function do have circadian components in CNS neurons. Limited direct evidence for CIPN chronotherapy.

## Myelosuppression (Brief)

### [Synthesized from available search data — primary mechanistic reference: Mathe G, Schwarzenberg L, and modern reviews; no single key paper confirmed at Key Paper tier for this bucket]

**Extraction (synthesized):**
- **Target cell:** Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) in bone marrow; megakaryocyte precursors (thrombocytopenia); erythroid precursors (anemia); myeloid progenitors (neutropenia/leukopenia)
- **Uptake transporters:** Cisplatin enters bone marrow progenitors via CTR1 and OCT-family transporters; platinum accumulation in HSPCs is proportional to cell sensitivity
- **DNA damage:** Cisplatin crosslinks rapidly dividing hematopoietic progenitors; intrastrand adducts → apoptosis of cycling progenitors; stem cell reserves depleted with cumulative dosing
- **ROS/mitochondria:** Oxidative stress contributes to HSPC apoptosis; mitochondrial dysfunction in rapidly dividing progenitors
- **Apoptosis:** Apoptosis of erythroid and megakaryocytic precursors; carboplatin (analog) JAK2 downregulation → megakaryocyte S-phase arrest → apoptosis (directly relevant mechanism for thrombocytopenia)
- **Inflammation:** [Bone marrow microenvironment disruption; secondary; limited primary inflammatory mechanism for cisplatin myelo]
- **Clinical endpoints:** CBC nadir (leukocytes/platelets) typically days 18–23 post-infusion; recovery by day 39; CTCAE Grade 3–4 neutropenia (<1.0 × 10⁹/L); cisplatin-associated anemia is partially mediated by renal erythropoietin deficiency (secondary to nephrotoxicity), not only direct bone marrow suppression; overall myelosuppression is MILD-MODERATE with cisplatin at standard head-and-neck doses (25–100 mg/m² q3w)
- **Reversibility:** Reversible between cycles; cumulative residual bone marrow injury and myelodysplastic changes with very high cumulative doses; anemia may persist due to EPO deficiency from nephrotoxicity
- **Circadian note:** STRONG CIRCADIAN RELEVANCE — bone marrow HSPCs have well-characterized circadian rhythms in cell-cycle progression (S-phase peak in light phase in mice; inverted in humans); hematopoietic toxicity is markedly circadian-dependent; cisplatin administered during HSPC G1/early S-phase nadir minimizes myelosuppression; foundational animal data support dosing at circadian times when progenitor cells are in G0/G1; this is among the strongest circadian arguments for cisplatin chronotherapy (see Lévi chronotherapy literature in Bucket 4)
